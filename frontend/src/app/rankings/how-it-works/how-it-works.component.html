How the rankings work

Every number here is computed from results: the professional games we hold from the LCK, LPL, LEC, LCS, CBLOL and LCP, plus the international events. There is no panel and no vote. This page covers what the numbers mean, what they cannot tell you, and where the model is weakest.

Boards are not comparable

A regional board ranks only against itself.

A player's regional rating is a percentile among the others playing the same role in that league, and every league's scale is centred on 50. A CBLOL player rated 78 and an LCK player rated 78 are each well above average for their own league. That is the whole of what the number says. It is not a shared standard and it does not travel.

Team ratings have the same limit for a different reason: a rating floats within the pool it plays in. Two regions can sit on the same numbers while playing to different standards, and nothing in a purely regional record would show it.

One board does compare regions: International. It is built only from games teams played against each other, at First Stand, MSI and Worlds. Everyone on it faced the same field, so those numbers mean the same thing wherever a team is from.

What a team's rating is

The core is Glicko-2, a rating system that tracks both a rating and how certain it is. A team's number is two terms:

  • Their own record. How they have done against everyone they have played, updated by every game. International games count double: they are the only cross-region evidence and there are about ten times fewer of them, so at equal weight a domestic schedule outvotes them.
  • Their region's strength, measured only from international results. For a team that never leaves its league this is the only cross-region signal there is.

Both are needed. Averaged by league, the first term barely separates them and does not even put them in the right order. Essentially all the cross-region information sits in the second.

A region's credit fades if it stops showing up: full for six months, tapering to a floor over two years. It never reaches zero, because a team that has not had the chance still belongs to its region.

Regional team boards are not cut to a recent window. Old results already count for very little, through the rating's own drift and the volume of regional play at 40 to 80 games a split. We tested cutting the record to 24, 18 and 12 months and graded every version on the same recent games. The differences were inside noise except at 12 months, which was measurably worse. Past a point a window only discards evidence.

The International board is cut, to the last six events, or about two years. International play runs two or three events a year, so without a hard cutoff a result from two seasons ago leaks in forever, and this board's claim is who is good at that level now. A team needs 10 games across those events to appear. One missing from it is not ranked low; it is not ranked.

The ± range

Every rating carries a range, and the range is the point. It is how far the model thinks the number could be out: narrow when a team has played a lot recently against known opposition, wide when we have seen little or the roster just changed.

A roster change widens the range without resetting it. We usually know the incoming players well enough to move the rating with them, but five known players are still an unknown combination, so some of the certainty goes. A team whose roster changed inside its last 10 games is flagged on the board.

On the player boards the same idea is drawn as a bar: solid out to what the games have earned, then a dashed tail on to where the rating would settle if the form holds. Hovering it gives Settled, Firming or Provisional.

Why a lower number can sit above a higher one

The teams board is ranked on Floor, which has its own column: the rating minus its uncertainty, or what we are confident a team is at least worth. Rating sits beside it as the single best estimate. Both are on screen, and either will sort the table.

Reading down the Rating column you will find the occasional pair out of order. A team carrying a high number on thin evidence has a low floor, and it ranks where its floor puts it. That is what ranking this way buys: a spectacular but barely-tested rating stays out of the top few places until the games back it up. The ± column between them shows how much evidence each one has.

The player boards have nothing like this. They are ranked on the number they show.

The ▲▼ arrows

The arrows are movement since a stated earlier date, printed next to “last updated”. They are not “since yesterday”, because a board does not advance daily.

A board advances a stage at a time, so days can pass without one moving. A regular-season week is held until it owes no more fixtures, so a team that played on Saturday does not leapfrog one playing on Sunday and then drop back a day later. Bracket play advances series by series, which is how it is resolved.

An arrow compares the board against how it stood at the end of an earlier stage, and the header names that date. Where there is nothing earlier to compare against it says so rather than drawing a row of dashes, because no arrow is better than one measured against itself.

Player ratings

A regional player rating is a percentile within a league and a role: 50 is the average player at that position in that league. Roles are rated separately because a support's box score and a mid laner's are not on the same scale.

The rating is a blend, weighted by role:

  • Win rate at 0.40, the player's share of the team's results.
  • Damage per minute at 0.20. It is the least team-dependent stat in the data, and the only lever we have found that lowers a rating's correlation with team strength without making the board less recognisable.
  • The rest of the box score for the remaining 0.40, reweighted per role: gold difference, CS per minute, gold and damage share, KDA, kill participation and objective control.

Why include win rate at all on a board meant to measure individuals? Good players genuinely do win more, and good teams recruit good players, so some correlation with team results is irreducible. Strip out every win-correlated term and the board ranks Faker 601st while predicting games about as well as a coin flip. Weight it much higher and the board becomes the league standings in different clothes. 0.40 is where a winning playmaker still outranks a losing stat-padder.

Games are weighted by recency, with a 120-day half-life. The board also offers three windows: All, This year and This split. They run the same method over fewer games. A split is only a few dozen games, so those ratings sit closer to 50. That narrower spread is the shrinkage working as intended. Both bounded windows key off each league's own split start, since the regions do not share a calendar.

A regional board carries who is in the league now: rostered to a team still playing there, or having played in it since the current split began. A player who changes region leaves the old board outright, since ranking them against players they no longer face answers nothing. A player arriving from another league is not treated as an unknown either. Their rating is pulled toward a carryover anchor about a third of the way from neutral, which is roughly what past-league standing turns out to be worth.

The International player board is a role-only pool of everyone with an international record, and it takes five international games to appear. That pool really did play each other, so those numbers do compare across regions.

Which games count

  • Regular season and playoffs, including Regional Finals: the separate post-season event the LPL runs a few days after its playoffs. It is playoff-grade and it counts.
  • Promotion play is excluded. A league team playing an outsider for a slot is not a season game, and rating it would let a result against a team on no board move a board.
  • Scheduled fixtures move nothing. We store them up to three weeks ahead, but only so a board can tell whether a week still owes games before it advances.
  • A finished game waits for its player stat lines before it moves any rating, so the team and player boards cannot drift apart over a publication lag. The wait is bounded: some games never get stat lines at all, and waiting forever would throw away real results. This is why a result can show on a team's page before it has moved the board.

Results, rosters and tournament data come from Liquipedia and are refreshed once a day. The ratings themselves are computed here.

Where the model is weakest

Every one of these is measured:

  • It is overconfident at the top end. Where it claims better than 80% it is claiming about 85% and delivering about 79%. Narrowing the ranges to tidy up the boards would make it worse: tighter ranges push the probabilities further out.
  • One number per region cannot represent an intransitive matchup. The LEC does better than the LCS against every large common opponent, yet the LCS beats the LEC head-to-head, repeatedly, across several events and different organisations. A single league rating has to pick one of those stories. Ours follows the common-opponent evidence, which has far more games behind it, and so under-rates the LCS.
  • Region gaps are overstated where play is thin. The model is honest where data is thick: its LCK–LPL gap predicts within half a point of the actual result over a hundred games. It is stretched at the CBLOL end, which has only a few dozen cross-region games behind it.
  • A player with no professional record anywhere gets nothing better than a neutral start. That is mostly academy call-ups and brand-new teams, and closing it takes tier-two data; a cleverer prior cannot do it.
